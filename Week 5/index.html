01 · The question

A link says two characters are connected. It never says how.

The week 4 communities were built from an unsigned, unlabelled graph. Spider-Man links to Green Goblin for the same structural reason he links to Mary Jane Watson: the page mentions them. If communities are really "social groups", you'd expect foes to end up apart. If they're really "storylines", foes should sit together — a hero and his nemesis appear in all the same comics.

So which is it — do communities cluster allies, or stories?

That's testable. Label the edges, then compare how often foe edges stay inside a community against how often ally edges do — and against what you'd get from chance alone.

02 · How the strings got their colour

One sentence per edge

  1. Pull the sentence. For every link A→B, find the sentence on A's page where B is mentioned. Resolve the link target, not just the display text, so [[Peter Parker|Spidey]] still points at the right page.
  2. Build a concordance. One line per edge, keyword in context. This is the corpus the rest of the analysis runs on — not the raw pages.
  3. Match a small word list. Lemmatise, then match against three sets: foe (enemy, nemesis, villain, fought, killed, defeated, rival), ally (ally, teammate, joined, partner, mentor, friend), family (married, wife, husband, son, daughter, brother, sister, father, mother).
  4. Resolve collisions. Many sentences match more than one set. Our rule is written down in the appendix and applied by code, not by eye.
  5. Audit a random sample by hand. This is the step that decides whether anything below is allowed to be called a finding.

What the word list cannot see

Negation (never fought), a third party in the sentence, a relationship that changed across decades, and anything stated on B's page but not A's. We measured how often this bites instead of assuming it doesn't — see section 04.

Why one sentence, not the page

A whole page mentions dozens of relationships. If you matched keywords against the full text you'd label every edge "foe" simply because the character is a hero with enemies somewhere on the page. The sentence is the smallest unit that still attaches a word to a specific pair.
