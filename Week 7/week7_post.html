Week 7 · Go nuts

Can a machine smell a villain?

We taught a model what Marvel villain pages sound like, using official labels and no hand-written examples. Then we used it to follow 191 superheroes' lives sentence by sentence. Do their stories have moral shapes?

The short version. The model learns something real: it ranks villains above heroes it has never seen (AUC 0.76). But it also learned gender from lopsided training data, and it responds to fights versus personal life more than to good versus evil deeds. Superhero lives do not fall into recurring shapes. On average, though, a biography drifts from "hero-sounding" toward "villain-sounding" text.

Building the villain detector

The idea: if villain pages and hero pages are written differently, a model can find the direction in meaning-space that points from "hero" to "villain". We call that direction the evilness axis, then measure any text along it, down to single sentences.

Labels from FiveThirtyEight's Marvel Wikia data: Good / Neutral / Bad, written by fans and not by us
160 labelled Wikipedia pages outside our 303: 89 Good, 39 Bad (incl. Thanos, Norman Osborn, Carnage)
Embed each page with MiniLM, in 180-word passages averaged into one vector
Train logistic regression, Good vs Bad. Its weight vector is the axis
Score our 303 pages and every biography sentence along it

The 303 course characters were never used in training, so testing on them is a fair out-of-sample check. Neutral characters were held out entirely and used only to see whether they land in the middle.

An AUC of 0.76 is useful but far from a lie detector: the groups overlap a lot. There are also only 11 "Bad" characters in a category of superheroes, so every number about villains rests on few examples. The comparison with the word list is on just 9 villains, so we don't call the difference significant.

What did it really learn?

A model always gives an answer. To see what the number means, read the extremes. These are the sentences, out of all 11,186 biography sentences, that sit furthest toward each end.

The villain end is full of battles, monsters and cosmic threats, often with heroes doing the fighting (Balder defending the gods). The hero end is full of relationships and everyday life. So the axis captures "reads like a villain page", and villain pages are mostly about conflict. It doesn't capture who does the bad deed.

The gender problem

In the training data, 49% of hero pages are mainly about a woman (more she/her than he/him), against only 15% of villain pages. A model can't tell "villain" from "male" when the two travel together. Each dot below is a course page: its share of female pronouns against its score.

The fix we used: find the direction in meaning-space that best predicts a page's pronoun share (an objective count, no labels involved), subtract it from every vector, and retrain. That cut the correlation from ρ = −0.36 to ρ = −0.20 without hurting accuracy (AUC 0.76 → 0.76). Gender is still detectable, so a single direction doesn't capture all of it. Every chart below uses the gender-removed axis.

Do superhero lives have shapes?

Our original hope was to find a few recurring moral journeys, like "fall from grace" or "redemption". We clustered all 191 arcs (k-means on the shape, with each arc's overall level removed) and asked whether the clusters are tighter than chance. "Chance" can be built two ways:

  • Shuffled: the same sentences in random order. That's an easy bar to beat.
  • Rotated: each biography's sentences shifted around a circle, so neighbouring sentences stay neighbours but their position in the story is scrambled. That's the fair bar.
No recurring shapes. The real arcs beat shuffled sentences, which only shows that neighbouring sentences are similar. Smoothing turns that into wiggles, and wiggles cluster. Against rotated arcs, which have the same wiggles, the real ones never do better. We found no evidence that Marvel lives fall into a handful of shapes.

…but there is a drift

Averaging all 191 arcs is a different question: is there a typical direction of travel? The grey band shows where the average would fall 95% of the time if story position didn't matter (300 rotated versions).

16 of 50 points fall outside the band. Biographies open on the hero side, and the second half sits on the villain side. Given what the axis responds to, the most careful reading is that biographies start with origins and personal life and move into conflict-heavy storylines. That is a claim about the text. It doesn't mean characters become more evil.

Did Marvel get darker?

Each dot is a course character, placed by the year of its first appearance (taken from the page's own "first appeared in…" sentence; found for 173 pages) and its page-level score.

If anything, the opposite: later characters score slightly less villain-like (Spearman ρ = −0.15, p = 0.043). That's weak. The trend explains very little, and with all the tests on this page, one p ≈ 0.04 shouldn't carry much weight. We'd call it "no sign of a darkening trend among these superheroes".

What we checked & where it breaks

  • No leakage: trained only on pages outside the 303, then tested on the 303.
  • Validation: cross-validation, an out-of-sample test, a held-out Neutral group, 100 bootstrap refits, and a comparison with a word-list baseline.
  • Bias: measured gender leakage, removed one gender direction, and reported what's left.
  • Null models: two nulls for arc shapes, with the fairer one deciding. A null band for the average arc.
  • Labels: fan-written Wikia labels from 2014, matched automatically to 160 of 303 pages (strict rules, no guessing).
  • Domain shift: the axis is trained on whole pages but applied to single sentences, which is a different kind of text.
  • Encoder limits: MiniLM reads at most 256 word pieces, so pages were cut into 180-word passages and averaged. That choice is part of the measurement.
The takeaway

You can teach a model what villain pages sound like, without writing a single example yourself, and it beats a word list. But "sounds like a villain page" turned out to mean fights, monsters and men as much as evil. That's a good reminder of the week's message: a model always returns a number, and the work is finding out what the number measures. The lives we tracked do drift from hero-sounding origins into villain-sounding conflict, but we found no small set of shared moral shapes.

Data: 02805 course snapshot of 303 Marvel superhero Wikipedia pages; 160 further Marvel character pages from Wikipedia; FiveThirtyEight comic-characters (Marvel Wikia, 2014). Encoder: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (Sentence Transformers). Classifier: scikit-learn logistic regression (balanced classes). Baseline: VADER. Tests: Mann–Whitney U, Spearman ρ, silhouette scores with 30 null resamples each, bootstrap and rotation nulls.
