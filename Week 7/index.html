Week 7 · Go nuts

Moral Arcs

Do Marvel characters' lives have a shape? We scored every sentence of 191 Wikipedia biographies and followed the mood from origin story to the latest issue. Then we checked how much that number can actually tell us.

The short version. A word list can draw a mood line through a character's life, and the lines are fun to read. But the patterns we found across all characters are weak or fragile, and the word list only loosely tracks Marvel's own good/bad labels. Mood, it turns out, is not the same thing as morality.
Finding 1 · fragile
Openings read a bit brighter

The first 10% of a biography scores higher than the last 10% (p = 0.024). With 20% or 33% windows, the gap is no longer significant.

Finding 2 · weak
Mood ≠ morality

Median sentiment goes Good > Neutral > Bad, as you'd hope. But only Good vs Bad clears p < 0.05 (p = 0.031), and there are just 9 "Bad" characters.

Finding 3 · no trend
Not steadily darker

Across 11,859 Marvel Wikia characters, the share of villains among each decade's newcomers goes up and down. There is no steady rise.

How we measured

Wikipedia character pages have a "Fictional character biography" section that retells the in-story life, roughly in order. That's the closest thing an encyclopedia has to a narrative, so it is the only part we score.

Cut out the biography section (279 of 303 pages have one)
Split into sentences; keep pages with ≥ 20 → 191 characters
Score each sentence with VADER, from −1 (negative) to +1 (positive)
Smooth with a moving average over 10% of the sentences
Stretch every arc to 50 points, so a 20-sentence life and a 300-sentence life line up

Official alignment labels (Good / Neutral / Bad) come from FiveThirtyEight's 2014 snapshot of the Marvel Wikia. We matched 160 of 303 pages with strict, automatic name rules and left the rest unlabelled rather than guess. No seed words, labels or examples were written by us.

The average life story

All 191 arcs averaged together, with a 95% bootstrap band. The band only says how sure we are of the average; individual lives scatter widely around it.

Almost fooled ourselves. Our first test (first 10% vs last 10% of sentences) said biographies end darker, p = 0.024. Then we changed the window size:

The result holds only for the narrowest window. The averages show why: the end sits around −0.11 to −0.13 whatever the window, while the very start is brighter. So the honest version is "the first few sentences read slightly more positive". Even that is a small effect, and we haven't tested why. One plausible reason is that origin stories open with births, families and new powers, but we didn't verify that.

Does the word list know who's good?

Each dot is one labelled character's average sentence score. Bars mark the median of each group. If mood measured morality, the three rows would separate cleanly.

GoodNeutralBad

They do line up in the expected order (medians −0.08, −0.13, −0.18), but the rows overlap almost completely. Good vs Bad: p = 0.031 (Mann–Whitney). Good vs Neutral: p = 0.085. With only 9 Bad and 22 Neutral characters in a category of superheroes, this is a hint, not a result.

Has Marvel gotten darker?

A different angle that uses the official labels directly: of all the characters Marvel introduced each decade, what share is labelled Bad? Hover for counts.

All labelled charactersOnly characters with ≥ 20 appearances

There's no steady march toward darkness. Among all characters, the villain share is highest for the 1940s and lowest for the 2000s. Among established characters (≥ 20 appearances), it peaks in the 1960s. Two reasons not to over-read this: the labels describe characters as of 2014, not at their debut, and a character created in 2005 had fewer years to reach 20 appearances than one from 1965.

What we checked & where it breaks

  • Robustness: re-ran the start-vs-end test with 3 window sizes × 3 minimum lengths (table above). Only 3 of 9 settings come out significant, all of them with the 10% window.
  • Label matching: automatic rules (exact title, real name in the opening sentence) and alternate-universe pages excluded. We read through the matched list, and the remaining doubtful cases are pages covering several people with one name (e.g. Red Guardian, Hyperion).
  • Instrument mismatch: VADER was built for social-media text. Wikipedia is written to be neutral, so most sentences score near 0.
  • Order: "Fictional character biography" is roughly chronological in-story time, not publication time, and retcons and alternate timelines scramble it.
  • Many tests: we ran several comparisons, so a single p ≈ 0.03 deserves little weight on its own.
The takeaway

A sentiment lexicon happily gives every sentence a number, and the lines it draws through a character's life are fun to explore. But a number isn't automatically a measurement. Across 191 Marvel biographies, the mood patterns we found are fragile, and they only loosely follow Marvel's own good/bad labels. A likely reason (which we didn't test): heroes' and villains' pages both describe fights, deaths and attacks, so both are full of dark words. If you want to measure who is good, word mood is the wrong instrument.
