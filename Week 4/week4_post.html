The stateless philosophers

We ran Louvain 200 times on the philosopher network. Most philosophers land in the same tradition every time. A few hundred keep switching, and they turn out to be the people who never fit in a philosophy department.

The week 4 page makes two points about Louvain. A single run is a sample, not the answer: two seeds give different partitions with almost the same Q. And one of the communities, the one named after Avicenna, puts the medieval Islamic and Jewish philosophers together with Galileo and Copernicus. Sune calls it "not a school anyone would name".

We wanted to know if those two observations are connected. So instead of reporting one partition, we treated 200 of them as data and asked a question about each philosopher: how often does Louvain put you with the same people? The bars above show the answer for three names. Kant lands among the German philosophers in 98% of runs. Galileo has no fixed home.

The map, coloured by how sure Louvain is

Step 1: is there anything to be unsure about?

Before measuring instability on the real network, we need to know what instability looks like when there is no structure at all. We ran Louvain with 200 seeds on the giant component (1,374 philosophers, 9,139 links). We also made 20 degree-preserving shuffles and ran 20 seeds on each.

  • Real network: 7 to 11 communities per run, Q from 0.472 to 0.510 (mean 0.501).
  • Shuffled: Q = 0.222 ± 0.002. The real mean sits about 120 standard deviations above it. (The course page reports 0.228; our shuffles differ in seed and swap count.)

That is the usual comparison. The more useful one is agreement between runs. On the real network, two runs agree at NMI 0.56 to 0.85. On a shuffled network they agree at 0.05 to 0.15. A shuffled network still gets a respectable Q, but no two runs agree on what the groups are.

Step 2: a consensus, and a loyalty score for everyone

For every pair of philosophers we counted the share of the 200 runs that put them together. Linking pairs that share a community in at least half the runs, then running Louvain on that weighted graph, gives a consensus partition. This is the standard consensus-clustering recipe (Lancichinetti and Fortunato, 2012).

The consensus has eight traditions, the same eight the course page names, plus nine philosophers in three tiny leftover groups (six Polish thinkers and three French ones). Its Q is 0.508, as good as the best single run. It is also reproducible: consensus from runs 1–100 and consensus from runs 101–200 agree at NMI 0.92, well above any pair of single runs.

Then each philosopher gets a loyalty score. In each run we label every community with the consensus tradition most of its members belong to. Loyalty is the share of runs in which a philosopher's community carries their own tradition's label.

  • 19% of philosophers are loyal in all 200 runs, and 60% in at least 90% of them.
  • 200 philosophers (15%) are loyal in fewer than 60% of runs. We call them stateless.
  • On the shuffled networks, mean loyalty is 0.18, against 0.87 on the real network (measured the same way, 20 runs at a time).

Step 3: the group that travels together

Low loyalty could mean two things. Stateless philosophers could scatter at random, or they could move as a group. The co-assignment counts show which:

  • Galileo and Kepler share a community in 94% of runs. Averaged over all pairs among Galileo, Kepler, Copernicus, Bruno, Mersenne, Ptolemy and Paracelsus, the figure is 72%.
  • Avicenna and Averroes are together in 89% of runs. The Islamic philosophers as a whole stay together in 80%.
  • What changes is who they are placed with. Galileo's community is labelled scholastic in 41% of runs, early modern in 33%, its own Avicenna–Galileo group in 16% and Greek antiquity in 8%.

The scientists of the Scientific Revolution are a stable group of philosophers without a stable tradition. Louvain attaches them either to the scholastics they were arguing with or to the early moderns who took up their work.

Their Wikipedia articles give a reason. They link backward to Aristotle, Ptolemy and the Arabic commentators (the scientists share a community with Aristotle in 59% of runs) and forward to Descartes and Newton (30% and 27%). Modularity has to put them on one side of that line, and the two sides score almost the same. That is a story we are telling about the data, not something the network proves. Section 4 tests it.

Step 4: are the stateless ones just the hubs?

The obvious objection is that big nodes link everywhere, so of course they move. But loyalty is unrelated to degree (Spearman ρ = 0.04). It is related to the participation coefficient, which measures how evenly a philosopher's links are spread across traditions (ρ = −0.47). What matters is how evenly the links are split, not how many there are.

Step 5: does seed instability predict where methods disagree?

If loyalty only measured Louvain's randomness, other algorithms would have no reason to disagree about the same philosophers. We checked two:

  • Infomap (map equation, 20 trials): 33 modules, NMI 0.73 against the consensus. We labelled each module by its majority tradition, so Infomap splitting a tradition into smaller modules does not count as disagreement.
  • Weighted Louvain (50 seeds, link weight = number of mentions, weighted Q = 0.558). A philosopher counts as moved if weighted Louvain puts them elsewhere in more than half the runs.

Both methods disagree with the consensus almost only about the stateless philosophers. Loyalty sorts the disagreements from the agreements with an AUC of 0.86 for both methods. Degree does worse than a coin flip (0.45).

Step 6: reading the pages (validation)

The right-hand panel above is our first validation. Among stateless philosophers, 38% are described as scientists, physicians or polymaths, against 11% of the loyal ones (odds ratio 5.1, Fisher p ≈ 10⁻¹⁷). That is not just the Galileo group: with the Avicenna–Galileo community removed, the numbers are still 28% against 11%. The keyword rule is a blunt instrument, so we checked it on 12 random matches. All 12 were real scientists or polymaths (Einstein, Euclid, Duhem, Teilhard de Chardin, Wallace…). It will miss scientists whose description uses other words, so the true gap is, if anything, understated.

Then we read the Wikipedia descriptions of the least loyal philosophers with degree 15 or more, and wrote down whether the split matches something real about the person:

Five of seven splits match the person's history. The other two, Durkheim and Darwin, are real too, but they reflect Wikipedia's link structure more than any school: the network has no sociology or biology community to put them in, so they get assigned to whichever philosophers cite them.

What we claim, and what we don't

We claim that Louvain's run-to-run disagreement on the philosophers is structured, not noise. It is concentrated in about 15% of philosophers, it picks out the same people other methods disagree about, and those people are disproportionately scientists and polymaths whose articles link across traditions. The Avicenna–Galileo community is not a school. It is the place where Louvain puts the people it cannot place anywhere else.

We don't claim that these philosophers "belong to" two traditions in any historical sense. The links are Wikipedia editors' choices. Loyalty also depends on our labelling rule (each run community gets the label of its majority tradition), and it is lowest in the one community that only exists as its own group in 83% of runs. An overlapping method (link communities) would be the natural next step: it can give Galileo two memberships instead of one uncertain one.

For the report writer: a single run of Louvain, reported as "the 9 schools", hides all of this. Report the consensus, report the NMI between runs, and report which nodes move.

Method, for anyone who wants to reproduce it

Pipeline and parameters
  • Data: course release week4_philosophers_*.tsv (frozen 15 Sep 2026). Directions summed, giant component: 1,374 nodes, 9,139 links.
  • Louvain: networkx.community.louvain_communities(G, seed=s, weight=None), s = 0…199. Weighted runs: weight="weight", s = 0…49.
  • Null: 20 × double_edge_swap(nswap=10m), 20 seeds each.
  • Co-assignment Pij = share of runs with i and j together. Consensus = Louvain on the graph of pairs with Pij ≥ 0.5, weighted by Pij.
  • Loyalty: per run, each community gets the consensus label of its majority; loyalty = share of runs where a node's community label equals its own consensus label.
  • Infomap 2.x, --two-level --num-trials 20 --seed 1, undirected, unweighted.
  • Backbone: disparity filter written from the formula, α = 0.2, kept if significant at either end. Checked against the course: 1,540 links, 950 philosophers, giant 816. Layout: spring layout of the backbone's giant component.
  • NMI: sklearn.metrics.normalized_mutual_info_score (arithmetic normalisation, as on the course page).
How we checked the machine's work
  • Network size, Q range, weighted Q and the backbone table all match the week 4 page before we used them.
  • The consensus reproduces across independent halves of the runs (NMI 0.92).
  • The loyalty score was run on shuffled networks: 0.18 there, 0.87 on the real network.
  • The keyword rule was checked by reading 12 random matches. The torn-philosopher table was checked by reading descriptions, not by asking a model.
Data: the 02805 philosophers release (Wikipedia, philosophers born before 1900). Numbers are from our run; seeds are listed above. Colours follow each philosopher's consensus tradition and are always paired with a label.
