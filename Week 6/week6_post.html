From counts to meaning

Week 6 — TF-IDF, topic models and word embeddings

Counting words is easy. Understanding why two pages — or two words — are similar is harder. This week we move from raw frequencies to weighted document vectors, recurring topics and context-based word representations, using the Marvel universe as our running example.

Can we recognize what a Marvel character is about from the words on their page — and what words mean from the company they keep?

Two kinds of meaning

There are two different objects in our analysis. First, documents are represented by the words they contain. Then, words are represented by the contexts in which they appear. Both approaches use numerical vectors and cosine similarity, but answer different questions.

01 / Document representations

Not all words deserve equal weight

A raw count vector is dominated by frequent words such as the and and. Those words do not help us distinguish Wolverine from Spider-Man. TF-IDF combines a word's frequency within a document with its rarity across the corpus.

TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × log(N / DF(t))

The more documents contain a term, the less informative it becomes. In the course's three-document example, the occurs in every document and receives IDF 0; mutant occurs in just one and receives a higher weight.

Similarity between two document vectors is measured using the angle between them, not their absolute length.

cos(A, B) = (A · B) / (||A|| ||B||)

For example, [1, 1] and [2, 2] have cosine similarity 1 because they point in the same direction, while [1, 1] and [1, 0] have similarity about 0.707. This makes cosine comparison less sensitive to differences in document length.

What changes for Marvel?

On the course's Marvel corpus, TF-IDF improves the agreement between the ten most similar text pages and their actual network neighbors. The teaching analysis reports an average of 4.0 linked pages out of ten with TF-IDF, compared with 2.8 after removing stopwords and 1.9 with raw counts.

Better weighting is not the same as understanding. Storm's closest text match can be the Human Torch because his name is Johnny Storm. TF-IDF sees a distinctive shared term, not the difference between a surname and a superpower.

A term's position suggests a difference in usage, but its interpretation requires reading the pages where it appears. A term can reflect a story, a shared character name, or even reused Wikipedia formatting.

03 / Recurring themes

Documents are mixtures of topics

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) models each topic as a probability distribution over words, and each document as a probability distribution over topics. The topic labels themselves are interpretations we give the model's results — not labels the algorithm knows in advance.

Explore an LDA topic mixture

Two illustrative document mixtures from the Week 6 teaching material; choose a document to see its topic proportions.

The same caution appears in the Marvel example. With names included, topics often become lists of names (Spider-Man, X-Men and associated characters). Removing likely names makes themes such as mutants, symbiotes, vampires and combat more visible — but also exposes recurring Wikipedia media boilerplate.

Preprocessing is part of the model. Changing which words the algorithm can see changes which topics it can discover. A topic is useful only when its terms and representative documents support a meaningful interpretation.

04 / Word representations

A word is known by its context

One-hot vectors have a separate coordinate for each word, so dog is just as far from puppy as from democracy. A word-context matrix instead counts which words appear near each target word. This is the distributional hypothesis: words that occur in similar contexts often have related meanings.

The size of the context window matters. A small window focuses on immediate neighbors; a large one can capture broader associations.

Context window in action

Sentence: “the puppy chased the ball through the park”

From raw counts to PPMI

Raw co-occurrence is dominated by common context words, just as raw document counts were. Pointwise mutual information (PMI) compares an observed word-context pair against how often it would occur if the two were independent. PPMI retains positive associations and sets negative values to zero.

PMI(w, c) = log₂[P(w, c) / (P(w)P(c))]
PPMI(w, c) = max(PMI(w, c), 0)

In the course's illustrative matrix, raw-count cosine similarity between dog and finance is 0.72, largely because both co-occur with the. After PPMI, the similarity falls to 0, while dog and puppy remain very close (0.96).

05 / Dense embeddings

Word2Vec learns by predicting context

Word2Vec replaces high-dimensional count-based word vectors with compact dense embeddings. Instead of constructing a full word-context matrix, it learns representations through a prediction task.

Skip-gram

Given a center word, predict the words likely to occur around it.

chased→puppythe

CBOW

Given the surrounding context, predict the center word.

puppythe→chased

Negative sampling makes training cheaper by comparing observed word-context pairs with a small sample of unlikely pairs. Similar contexts can lead to similar learned vectors.

These vectors can support analogy-like operations, such as king − man + woman ≈ queen in some pretrained embeddings. Such examples show regularities in the learned space, not a perfect semantic understanding.

Corpus matters

On Marvel text, the course's trained Word2Vec example places mjolnir and odinson near hammer, while a pretrained general-language GloVe model associates hammer more strongly with other tools. Neither model has a context-free definition of meaning: its associations reflect the language on which it was trained.

What we learned

Frequency is not meaning. TF-IDF helps find distinctive terms, LDA uncovers patterns of co-occurring words across documents, and PPMI and Word2Vec model relationships between individual words.

Yet every representation inherits choices about preprocessing, context and training data. A close match is a hypothesis to investigate in the source text, not automatically a meaningful relationship.
