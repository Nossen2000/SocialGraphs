Compared to What?

The Marvel network looks special — high clustering, giant hubs, a heavy-tailed degree distribution. But every one of those claims needs a baseline. We put the network through three null models and found out what's real, what's mechanical, and what disappears when you shuffle.

The degree face-off

Does the Marvel network look more random, more BA, or neither? We compare CCDFs.

A CCDF plots P(K ≥ k) — the probability that a random node has at least degree k. On log-log axes, a power law becomes a straight line, a Poisson rolls over and dies, and the real data tells you which world it lives in.

The Marvel CCDF (gold) tracks the BA model (blue) down to roughly k ≈ 30, then the tail frays — finite-size noise with only 277 nodes. The random G(n,m) curve (coral) rolls over sharply: no hubs, no contest. The tail is heavy. Whether it's precisely a power law, we cannot say — 277 nodes is not enough to distinguish power law from lognormal or stretched exponential without formal testing (Clauset-Shalizi-Newman).
We can say the distribution is heavy-tailed — far more high-degree nodes than a random network would ever produce. We cannot say it follows a power law with a specific γ. That's the honest claim.

Your friends have more friends than you

The friendship paradox among Marvel superheroes.

Pick a random hero. Then pick a random one of their friends. On average, the friend has more connections than you. The gap is entirely determined by the variance of the degree distribution: the friend's expected degree is ⟨k⟩ + σ²/⟨k⟩.

The friend distribution (coral) has a long right tail that the person distribution (gold) doesn't. When you "pick a random friend," you're sampling nodes in proportion to their degree. Spider-Man, at degree 73, appears as someone's random friend 73× more often than a degree-1 hero.

In the random G(n,m) counterpart (same nodes and edges, Poisson degrees), the gap shrinks to about +1: σ² = ⟨k⟩ for Poisson, so σ²/⟨k⟩ = 1. The paradox still exists but it's barely noticeable. The heavy tail in Marvel makes it dramatic.

The shuffle reckoning

Which properties survive a degree-preserving shuffle? The survivors are mechanical; whatever dies is the real structure.

We generated 200 configuration-model shuffles (same degree sequence, randomly rewired) and 200 Erdős–Rényi G(n,m) baselines (same edge count, no degree structure). For each, we measured clustering. Then we asked: is the real value inside the null distribution, or miles outside it?

Average clustering C — 200 configuration-model shuffles
The real clustering (gold dashed line, 0.31) is 37 standard deviations to the right of the configuration-model distribution. Not even close. The degree sequence explains some clustering (config model gives 0.096 vs G(n,m)'s 0.035), but two thirds of the real clustering comes from structure beyond degrees.

Notice the gap between the configuration model (0.096) and edge swaps (0.128). Both are supposed to sample the same null, but the config model's stub-matching creates ~81 self-loops and parallel edges that get dropped — disproportionately losing hub edges and deflating clustering. Edge swaps preserve the exact edge count, so they run slightly higher.

What survived the shuffle: The friendship paradox, the heavy-tailed CCDF, and the giant component all survive — they depend on the degree distribution, not on specific wiring.

What died: Two thirds of the real clustering is destroyed by shuffling. The network's triangles require triadic closure and community structure that random wiring can't produce.

The degree sequences are remarkably similar at the top — both have a Spider-Man-scale hub in the 70s. But BA's clustering (0.108) is a third of the real value (0.310), because preferential attachment creates stars, not triangles. The model captures "who gets famous" but not "who hangs out together."

No single simple model gets all three pillars — hubs, clustering, and short paths — simultaneously. Erdős–Rényi gets distances. Watts–Strogatz gets clustering. Barabási–Albert gets hubs. The real Marvel network has all three, which means its generating process is richer than any one model can express.

What we learned

Every interesting claim about a network is implicitly a comparison. This week we learned to ask "compared to what?" and to match the null model to the claim.

The Marvel network's average clustering (C = 0.31) far exceeds what its degree sequence alone would produce: across 200 configuration-model randomisations preserving the exact degree sequence, the mean clustering was 0.096 ± 0.006, placing the real value more than 37 standard deviations above the null. The heavy-tailed degree distribution accounts for roughly half the gap between Erdős–Rényi (C ≈ 0.035) and the observed value, but the remaining threefold excess over the configuration model indicates that the network's clustering is driven by mechanisms beyond degree heterogeneity — most likely triadic closure and community structure among editorially related characters.

That paragraph — with the right null, the right comparison, and the right conclusion — is the kind of statement that belongs in a research paper. Everything else is decoration.
